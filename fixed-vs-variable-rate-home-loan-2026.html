Should you fix your interest rate or go variable? In 2026, with interest rates having moved significantly in recent years, this decision matters more than ever. Here's what you need to know — and what Sydney borrowers are actually doing right now.

Variable Rate Home Loans — Pros and Cons

  • PROS: Flexibility for extra repayments, offset accounts typically available, rate drops pass through immediately, no break costs if you refinance
  • CONS: Repayment uncertainty, potential for payment shock if rates rise, harder to budget around

Fixed Rate Home Loans — Pros and Cons

  • PROS: Complete repayment certainty, immune to rate rises during fixed term, easy to budget
  • CONS: Potentially large break costs if you exit early, limited extra repayments, offset accounts rarely available, miss rate drops

The Split Loan — Best of Both Worlds

A split loan divides your mortgage between fixed and variable portions — for example, 60% fixed and 40% variable. This gives you rate certainty on the majority of your debt while maintaining flexibility for extra repayments and offset access.

In 2026, split loans are increasingly popular among Sydney buyers who want protection against rising rates without surrendering variable loan flexibility.

What Are Sydney Buyers Choosing in 2026?

In 2026, with the RBA having moved through a significant rate cycle, most Sydney borrowers are favouring variable rates or short fixed periods of 1–2 years — primarily to avoid locking in and potentially missing further rate reductions.

However, for first home buyers stretching their repayment capacity, a partial or full fix may still provide important budget certainty.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are break costs on a fixed rate loan?

If you exit a fixed rate loan early — through selling, refinancing, or paying off — lenders charge break costs to compensate for lost interest income. Break costs can run into tens of thousands of dollars.

Can I have an offset account with a fixed rate loan?

Rarely. Most lenders don't allow offset accounts on fixed rate loans — a key reason many borrowers keep at least a portion of their loan on variable.

Should I wait to see if rates drop more before fixing?

Trying to perfectly time interest rates is notoriously difficult. The better question is: what repayment certainty do I need right now, and what can I afford if rates move in either direction?
